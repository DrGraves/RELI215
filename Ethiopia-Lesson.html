Lesson Overview

Many people imagine Christianity traveling from Jerusalem to Rome and then to Europe. But that is only one road.

Another road ran south, along the Red Sea and the Nile, into the highlands of northeast Africa. There, in the kingdom of Aksum (in what is now Ethiopia and Eritrea), Christianity became the faith of the royal court in the fourth century, earlier than in most of Europe.

Ethiopian Christians went on to preserve their own Bible, their own sacred language (Ge’ez), and their own architecture. Some of the most remarkable churches in the world were not built up from the ground. They were carved down into solid rock, in the town of Lalibela and on the cliffs and mountains of the Tigray region.

In this lesson, we will ask:

Question 1Where does Ethiopia appear in the Bible itself?
Question 2How and when did Christianity take root in Ethiopia?
Question 3What do Lalibela and the Tigray churches tell us about how Ethiopian Christians understood holy space?

Part 1

Ethiopia in the Bible and the Early Church

1. Ethiopia in the Biblical World

Ethiopia is not a stranger to the Bible. In the Hebrew Bible, the land south of Egypt is usually called Cush. Greek translators rendered it as Aithiopia, and English Bibles often say “Ethiopia.”

One caution: the biblical “Cush” mostly points to the ancient Nubian kingdom along the Nile (today’s Sudan), not exactly to the modern country of Ethiopia. Still, Ethiopian Christians have long read these passages as part of their own story.

Passages to know:

  • Psalm 68:31“Let Cush hasten to stretch out its hands to God.”
  • Numbers 12:1Moses marries a Cushite woman
  • Jeremiah 38:7–13Ebed-melech the Cushite rescues Jeremiah
  • Amos 9:7“Are you not like the Cushites to me?”
  • 1 Kings 10:1–13The Queen of Sheba visits Solomon

Key Tradition: The Queen of Sheba

Ethiopian tradition identifies the Queen of Sheba as an Ethiopian queen, called Makeda.

A fourteenth-century Ethiopian work, the Kebra Nagast (“Glory of the Kings”), tells that Makeda and Solomon had a son, Menelik I, who brought the Ark of the Covenant to Ethiopia. Ethiopian emperors claimed descent from this line (the “Solomonic dynasty”) until 1974.

Think About It

Psalm 68:31 became a favorite verse of Black American preachers in the 1800s and 1900s. Thinking back to Week 14, why might a verse about Ethiopia “stretching out her hands to God” have meant so much to people of African descent in America?

2. The Ethiopian Official (Acts 8)

In Acts 8:26–40, Philip meets a traveler on the desert road from Jerusalem to Gaza. The man is described as:

  • an Ethiopian
  • a eunuch
  • a court official
  • treasurer of the Candace (queen)
  • a worshiper returning from Jerusalem

He is reading Isaiah 53 aloud in his chariot. Philip explains the passage, and the official asks, “Look, here is water! What is to prevent me from being baptized?”

He becomes one of the first non-Jewish converts recorded in Acts.

Notice where this story sits: it comes before the conversion of Paul (Acts 9) and before the Roman centurion Cornelius (Acts 10). In the shape of Acts, the gospel reaches Africa early.

What the text says“Candace” (Kandake) was the title of queens of Kush, the Nubian kingdom centered at Meroë. Historically, the official was most likely Nubian.
How the story was receivedEthiopian Christians embraced him as a founding figure of their faith. Ethiopian Orthodox tradition remembers him as an early bearer of the gospel to Africa.
An important idea for this lesson: A community can claim a biblical story as its own, even when historians debate the exact geography.

3. Aksum Becomes Christian

In the fourth century, Aksum was a major trading power. It minted its own coins and traded across the Red Sea with Rome, Arabia, and India.

According to the church historian Rufinus, two young Christian brothers from Tyre, Frumentius and Aedesius, were shipwrecked on the Red Sea coast and brought to the Aksumite court. Frumentius became a trusted advisor and tutor to the young prince Ezana.

Frumentius later traveled to Alexandria, where Bishop Athanasius consecrated him as the first bishop of Aksum. Ethiopians remember him as Abba Salama, “Father of Peace.”

c. 330s CEKing Ezana adopts Christianity
1,600+ yrsEthiopian bishops linked to Alexandria (until 1959)
Late 400sThe “Nine Saints” arrive and spread monasticism

Stone inscriptions from Ezana’s reign show the shift: earlier ones honor traditional gods, while later ones speak of the “Lord of Heaven” and the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

Key Idea

Aksum was one of the first kingdoms in the world to make Christianity its official faith.

Christianity in Ethiopia is not a later import from European missionaries. It is older than Christianity in England, Germany, or Russia.

4. A Bible of Its Own

The Ethiopian church is known today as the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church. Tewahedo is a Ge’ez word meaning “made one,” referring to its belief in the united nature of Christ.

Beginning in the Aksumite period, the scriptures were translated into Ge’ez, the classical language of Ethiopia. Ge’ez is still used in worship today, much as Latin was once used in the Roman Catholic Mass.

Protestant Bible66 books
Roman Catholic Bible73 books
Ethiopian Orthodox Bible81 books (the traditional count)

The Ethiopian canon includes books such as 1 Enoch and Jubilees. These ancient Jewish writings were known to early Christians (Jude 14–15 quotes Enoch), but they survive in complete form only in Ge’ez. Without Ethiopian monks copying them for centuries, they might have been lost.

Ethiopian monasteries also preserve the Garima Gospels, illuminated Gospel books that radiocarbon testing places among the oldest surviving illustrated Christian manuscripts in the world.

The Ark and the Tabot

Every Ethiopian Orthodox church holds a tabot, a consecrated tablet representing the Ark of the Covenant.

A building is not considered a church until a tabot is placed inside. Tradition holds that the original Ark rests in the Church of Our Lady Mary of Zion in Aksum, guarded by a single monk. This shows how closely Ethiopian Christianity connects itself to the story of ancient Israel.

Think About This

Who decides which books belong in “the Bible”? What does the Ethiopian canon teach us about the idea that there is only one Bible?

Part 2

Churches Carved From Stone

5. Lalibela: A New Jerusalem

In the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, the Zagwe dynasty ruled the Ethiopian highlands. Its most famous king, Gebre Meskel Lalibela, is remembered for an extraordinary project: a group of eleven churches cut from living rock in the town that now bears his name.

Tradition says Lalibela wanted to create a “New Jerusalem.” Around this time, pilgrimage to the real Jerusalem had become much harder for Ethiopian Christians, after Saladin captured the city in 1187.

So the holy city was brought home. The landscape itself was renamed:

  • a stream called the “Jordan River”
  • a hill called “Golgotha”
  • a church of “Golgotha” and a symbolic “Tomb of Adam”
  • a “Mount Sinai” chapel
11monolithic and rock-cut churches
1978named a UNESCO World Heritage Site
~12 mdepth of the trench around Bete Giyorgis

The largest, Bete Medhane Alem (“House of the Savior of the World”), is often called the largest monolithic church in the world. The most famous, Bete Giyorgis (Church of St. George), is shaped like a cross when seen from above.

Lalibela is not a museum. Priests still lead worship there every day, and pilgrims dressed in white still fill its courtyards on feast days.

An important idea for this lesson: Lalibela shows a Christian community re-creating sacred geography, turning a biblical map into a place people could walk.

6. How Do You Carve a Church?

Most buildings are built up: stone is stacked on stone. The Lalibela churches were made the opposite way.

They were carved down, and from the outside in.

Workers cut deep trenches into the volcanic rock (a soft red tuff), leaving a giant block standing free. Then they hollowed the block from the top down, shaping columns, arches, windows, and roofs as they went. There was no room for a major mistake: you cannot add stone back.

The churches are connected by trenches, tunnels, and passageways. Some, like Bete Giyorgis, are fully freestanding (“monolithic”). Others remain attached to the rock on one or more sides.

LegendEthiopian tradition says angels worked through the night, continuing the work that human laborers did by day.
HistoryScholars think the complex was carved over a long period, possibly with some parts older than King Lalibela, by skilled workers using iron tools.

Think About It

Why might a community choose to carve a church out of the earth itself rather than build one on top of it? What might that say about permanence, humility, or the connection between creation and worship?

7. The Mountain Churches of Tigray

Lalibela is the most famous site, but it is not alone. In the Tigray region of northern Ethiopia, there are more than 120 rock-hewn churches. Many are carved into cliffs and mountaintops, especially in the Gheralta mountains.

Some of these churches may be older than Lalibela. Researchers still debate their dates, but several are thought to go back many centuries, and local tradition connects some to the Aksumite era.

Abuna Yemata Guh Carved into a sandstone pinnacle in the Gheralta range. To reach it, visitors climb a sheer rock face barefoot using handholds, then cross a narrow ledge with a long drop below. Inside, the domed ceilings are covered with remarkably preserved paintings of saints and apostles.
Debre Damo A monastery on a flat-topped mountain associated with Abuna Aregawi, one of the Nine Saints (late 400s–500s). The only entrance is a rope climb up a cliff about 15 meters high. By tradition, only men may enter.
Maryam Korkor & Daniel Korkor High on a Gheralta ridge, reached by a steep hike. Maryam Korkor holds wall paintings and carved pillars; tiny Daniel Korkor nearby was a hermit’s cell with a sweeping view of the valley.

Why build churches in such hard-to-reach places?

  • Monastic withdrawal: hermits and monks sought solitude for prayer, following the desert monastic tradition of Egypt and Syria.
  • Protection: high, hidden churches were safer from raids and conflict, and they protected manuscripts and sacred objects.
  • Biblical imagery: mountains are places of encounter with God: Moses on Sinai (Exodus 19), Elijah on Horeb (1 Kings 19), Jesus on the mountain (Matthew 5; 17).
  • The climb as prayer: the difficult journey itself becomes an act of devotion and humility.

Think About This

In Lalibela, holy space is carved down into the earth. In Tigray, many churches are carved up on the heights. What different ideas about meeting God might each one express?

8. Living Heritage, Present Danger

These churches are not ruins. They are active places of worship, cared for by priests, monks, and local communities who have protected them for centuries.

That heritage has also faced real threats:

  • Weather and time: rain and erosion wear down the soft rock at Lalibela. Large protective shelters were installed over several churches in the 2000s, and conservation work continues.
  • War: during the 2020–2022 conflict in Tigray, human rights organizations reported the killing of civilians in Aksum, and there were reports of damage to churches, monasteries, and manuscripts in the region. Lalibela itself changed hands during the fighting in 2021, raising international concern for the site.

Scholars and church leaders are still documenting what was lost. For many Ethiopians, these losses are not only about buildings, but about communities, memory, and faith.

Key Idea

Sacred places hold history, but they are also held by people.

When we study these churches, we are studying a living tradition, not just ancient architecture.
